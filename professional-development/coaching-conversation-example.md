## The Scenario

**Setting**: Weekly one-on-one meeting between Sarah (manager/coach) and Alex (team member)

**Context**: Alex requested this meeting, saying "I need to talk about some challenges." Sarah knows Alex is working on a major product launch and managing a small team.

**Time**: 15 minutes scheduled

---

## The Complete Conversation

### Opening (0:00)

**Sarah**: Thanks for making time, Alex. So, **what's on your mind?**

*[KICKSTART QUESTION - Opens the conversation, lets them choose what matters]*

**Alex**: Well, the product launch is coming up in three weeks and I'm feeling really overwhelmed. There's just so much to do.

**Sarah**: *(Resists urge to jump in with solutions, takes a breath)* Okay, **and what else** is on your mind about this?

*[AWE QUESTION #1 - First answer is rarely the only answer]*

**Alex**: The timeline is really tight. Marketing needs final specs by Friday, but the engineering team is still fixing bugs.

**Sarah**: **And what else?**

*[AWE QUESTION #2 - Going deeper]*

**Alex**: *(Pauses)* Honestly, I'm not sure my team is ready for this. They keep coming to me with questions instead of figuring things out themselves.

**Sarah**: **And what else?**

*[AWE QUESTION #3 - One more layer]*

**Alex**: *(Longer pause)* I guess... I'm worried I'm going to drop something important. I'm trying to coordinate between engineering, marketing, and the customer success team, and it feels like I'm the only one who sees the whole picture.

**Sarah**: **And what else?**

*[AWE QUESTION #4 - Checking if there's more]*

**Alex**: *(Sighs)* Actually, that's it. Those are the main things.

---

### Exploring the Challenge (3:00)

**Sarah**: Okay, so you've got the tight timeline, team readiness concerns, and feeling like you're the only one connecting all the dots. Of those challenges, **what's the real challenge here for you?**

*[FOCUS QUESTION - Cuts to what matters most to them personally]*

**Alex**: *(Thinks for a moment)* I think... it's that I'm trying to do everything myself. I'm the bottleneck.

**Sarah**: Say more about that.

*[Open-ended prompt, staying curious]*

**Alex**: Every decision runs through me. Marketing asks me about specs instead of asking engineering. The customer success team asks me about timelines instead of checking the project plan. My own team members ask me to solve their problems instead of trying solutions first.

**Sarah**: **And what else** is happening when you're the bottleneck?

*[AWE QUESTION #5 - Applied to this specific issue]*

**Alex**: I'm working nights and weekends. I'm stressed. And ironically, I'm probably slowing everything down because people have to wait for me to respond.

**Sarah**: *(Notices the urge to say "You should delegate more" but holds back)* Mm-hmm. **And what else?**

*[AWE QUESTION #6 - The Advice Monster is strong here, but she resists]*

**Alex**: I think people aren't developing. If I keep solving everything, they don't learn how to do it themselves.

---

### The 3P Framework (5:30)

**Sarah**: Let me ask - is this challenge more about the **project** (the launch itself), the **people** (your team or stakeholders), or a **pattern** you're noticing?

*[3P FRAMEWORK - Going deeper to find the real issue]*

**Alex**: *(Pauses thoughtfully)* That's a good question. I think... it's a pattern. This happened with the last launch too. And the one before that.

**Sarah**: What pattern do you see?

**Alex**: I take on too much. I think "I'll just do it myself, it'll be faster" and then I end up overwhelmed and become the bottleneck. And then I resent people for depending on me... even though I created that dependency.

**Sarah**: *(Wants to say "You're a classic Tell-It and Save-It Advice Monster victim!" but stays curious)* **And what else** do you notice about this pattern?

*[AWE QUESTION #7 - Exploring the pattern deeper]*

**Alex**: *(Long pause)* I think I don't trust that other people will do it as well as I would. Or that they'll understand all the moving pieces like I do.

---

### Clarifying Outcomes (7:00)

**Sarah**: So we're talking about a pattern of taking on too much, becoming the bottleneck, and not trusting others to handle the complexity. If we could wave a magic wand, **what do you want?**

*[FOUNDATION QUESTION - Clarifying desired outcome]*

**Alex**: I want to not be working nights and weekends. I want to feel less stressed.

**Sarah**: Okay. **And what else do you want?**

*[AWE + FOUNDATION combined - Finding the deeper desire]*

**Alex**: *(Thinks)* I want my team to be able to solve more problems without me. I want them to grow and become more independent.

**Sarah**: **And what else?**

**Alex**: I want the launch to actually go well. *(Laughs)* That would be nice too.

**Sarah**: So you want to reduce your workload, develop your team's independence, and still have a successful launch. **And what else do you want?**

*[Checking for hidden desires]*

**Alex**: *(Pauses)* Honestly? I want to prove that I can lead a major launch without burning out. Last time I did it, but I was exhausted for a month afterward. I want to do it differently this time.

**Sarah**: That's the real goal - lead successfully *and* sustainably.

**Alex**: Yes. Exactly.

---

### Defining Support (9:00)

**Sarah**: Okay. So given all of that - the pattern you're seeing, what you want to achieve - **how can I help?**

*[LAZY QUESTION - Prevents assuming what help they need]*

**Alex**: *(Thinks)* I'm not sure. Maybe... could you help me think through what I should actually keep doing versus what I should delegate?

**Sarah**: I can definitely do that. Is that the most useful thing, or is there something else?

*[Clarifying the request]*

**Alex**: No, I think that's it. I need help figuring out what only I can do, and what others should be doing.

**Sarah**: Alright. Before we dig into that - **is there anything else I can do for you?**

*[Critical follow-up to the Lazy Question]*

**Alex**: No, that's it.

**Sarah**: Good. So let me make sure I understand. You want to identify what you need to keep doing versus delegate, specifically for this launch, so you can reduce your bottleneck role. Is that right?

**Alex**: Yes.

---

### Working Through Solutions (10:00)

**Sarah**: Okay. So thinking about this launch - what are you currently doing that you think only you can do?

*[Open question, letting them think through it]*

**Alex**: The final decision-making on feature priorities. Communicating the overall vision and strategy. And making sure all the teams are aligned.

**Sarah**: **And what else** only you can do?

*[AWE applied to this specific question]*

**Alex**: *(Thinks)* Actually... maybe that's it? When I think about it, most of the other stuff - answering spec questions, resolving timeline questions, solving technical problems - other people could do those.

**Sarah**: So if you focused on those three things - final decisions, vision communication, and alignment - and delegated the rest, what would have to happen?

**Alex**: I'd need to make it clear to people where they should go for different questions. Like, technical questions go to the lead engineer, not to me. Timeline questions go to the project manager.

**Sarah**: What else would need to happen?

**Alex**: *(Pause)* I'd need to actually let them make decisions. Not step in when they're solving a problem, even if I think I could solve it faster.

**Sarah**: *(Feels the urge to say "Yes! And you should set up a RACI matrix!" but holds back)* And what would help you do that?

*[Staying in question mode, not advice mode]*

**Alex**: Maybe I could tell the team explicitly - "Unless it's a final feature decision, a strategic question, or a major alignment issue, solve it without me first. Try your best solution, and we can review it later if needed."

**Sarah**: How would that change things?

**Alex**: They'd have to step up. And I'd have to be okay with things not being done exactly how I would do them.

**Sarah**: *(Wants to validate this insight but stays curious)* What's hard about that?

**Alex**: *(Laughs)* Letting go of control. Trusting that it'll be good enough even if it's not perfect.

---

### Exploring Trade-offs (12:30)

**Sarah**: So if you're saying yes to delegating more and letting go of control, **what are you saying no to?**

*[STRATEGIC QUESTION - Making trade-offs explicit]*

**Alex**: *(Thinks hard)* I'm saying no to doing things my way every time. I'm saying no to being involved in every decision.

**Sarah**: **And what else** are you saying no to?

*[AWE + STRATEGIC combined]*

**Alex**: *(Long pause)* I'm saying no to the comfort of knowing exactly what's happening all the time. That's actually harder than I thought. I like being in control.

**Sarah**: What's important about being in control?

**Alex**: *(Reflects)* It makes me feel competent. Needed. Like I'm adding value.

**Sarah**: And if you delegate more, do you stop being competent or adding value?

*[Gentle challenge, still in question form]*

**Alex**: *(Pause)* No. Actually, developing my team and leading strategically is probably more valuable than answering individual questions. I just... haven't seen it that way before.

**Sarah**: So by saying yes to delegation and strategic leadership, you're saying no to the old way you proved your value. But you're saying yes to a new way - developing others and focusing on what only you can do.

**Alex**: Yeah. That's exactly it.

---

### Commitment and Next Steps (14:00)

**Sarah**: What's one thing you're going to do in the next 48 hours based on this conversation?

*[Concrete action - not one of the seven questions but important for accountability]*

**Alex**: I'm going to have a team meeting tomorrow and tell them explicitly what I'm keeping versus what I'm delegating. And I'll ask them to try solving things first before bringing them to me.

**Sarah**: What might get in the way of that?

**Alex**: *(Thinks)* My instinct to jump in when someone brings me a problem. I'll need to catch myself and ask them "What have you already tried?" instead of just solving it.

**Sarah**: *(Recognizing the AWE question in Alex's plan, smiles)* Good. Anything else?

**Alex**: I'll also email the other teams - marketing, customer success - and let them know who to go to for different types of questions instead of defaulting to me.

**Sarah**: When will you do that?

**Alex**: I'll send that email today before end of day.

---

### Closing - The Learning Question (14:45)

**Sarah**: Okay, we're almost out of time. Before we wrap up - **what was most useful for you in this conversation?**

*[LEARNING QUESTION - Always end with this]*

**Alex**: *(Pauses to think)* Realizing that this is a pattern, not just a one-time launch problem. I keep creating dependency by solving everything myself.

**Sarah**: *(Stays silent, letting it land)*

**Alex**: And recognizing that being in control makes me feel valuable, but it's not actually the most valuable thing I could be doing. That's... big. I need to think about that more.

**Sarah**: *(Wants to add "And you should also think about..." but resists)*

**Sarah**: Great. Thanks, Alex.

*[Ends cleanly, no additional advice, lets the insight be the last thing]*

**Alex**: Thanks, Sarah. This was really helpful.

## What Just Happened: Analysis

### The Seven Questions in Action

| Question | When Used | What It Accomplished |
|----------|-----------|---------------------|
| **What's on your mind?** | Opening | Started conversation, let Alex choose the topic |
| **And what else?** | Used 7 times throughout | Uncovered deeper issues (team dependency, personal control needs) |
| **What's the real challenge here for you?** | After initial exploration | Shifted from external problems to personal challenge (being the bottleneck) |
| **What do you want?** | Midway through | Clarified true goals (sustainable success, team development) |
| **How can I help?** | Before problem-solving | Defined specific, bounded support (delegation planning) |
| **If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?** | During solution discussion | Made visible the trade-off (control vs. strategic leadership) |
| **What was most useful for you?** | Closing | Extracted the learning (pattern recognition, redefining value) |

### Key Moments

**Minute 1-2: The Advice Monster Attack**
When Alex said "the team keeps coming to me with questions," Sarah felt a strong urge to say "You need to delegate!" But she asked "And what else?" instead. This led to the real issue surfacing.

**Minute 5: The 3P Framework**
Sarah shifted from Project (the launch) to Pattern (taking on too much). This is where the conversation went deep.

**Minute 7: The Hidden Desire**
"What else do you want?" revealed the real goal wasn't just a successful launch - it was proving Alex could lead without burning out.

**Minute 12: The Deeper Trade-off**
"What are you saying no to?" uncovered that control = feeling valuable. This insight wouldn't have emerged without the Strategic Question.

**Minute 14: The Learning**
Alex identified the pattern and the underlying belief. This is what they'll remember and work on long after the conversation.

### The Transformation

**Before the conversation**:
- Alex focused on external problems (timeline, team, coordination)
- Feeling overwhelmed and stuck
- Reactive mode

**After the conversation**:
- Alex recognized internal pattern (taking on too much to feel valuable)
- Has specific actions to take (team meeting, email)
- Shifted to strategic thinking

**Sarah's role**:
- Asked questions, didn't give advice
- Stayed curious through discomfort
- Let Alex find their own solutions
- Extracted the learning

## The Traditional Approach (For Comparison)

### How This Would Have Gone Without Coaching Questions

**Alex**: The product launch is coming up and I'm feeling really overwhelmed. There's just so much to do.

**Sarah**: *[ADVICE MONSTER ACTIVATED]* Oh, I know exactly what you need to do. You need to delegate more. You're taking on too much.

**Alex**: Yeah, but the team keeps asking me questions...

**Sarah**: That's because you haven't set clear expectations. What you should do is create a RACI matrix showing who's responsible for what. Also, set up office hours - tell people they can only bring you questions during specific times.

**Alex**: I guess, but...

**Sarah**: And you need to empower your team more. Stop solving their problems for them. When they come to you, ask them what they've already tried.

**Alex**: Okay...

**Sarah**: Also, you should block out focus time on your calendar. Don't let people interrupt you. And make sure you're prioritizing ruthlessly - not everything is urgent.

**Alex**: Right. Thanks.

*[Conversation ends]*

### What's Wrong with This Approach?

**For Sarah**:
- She just took on Alex's problem
- Alex will come back with the next problem
- She's created dependency, not capability

**For Alex**:
- Didn't discover the pattern themselves
- No ownership of the solution
- Didn't uncover the underlying belief about control and value
- Less likely to actually implement (not their idea)
- Didn't learn how to think through this type of problem

**The outcome**: Advice given, but no real change. The pattern continues.

## Key Techniques Demonstrated

### 1. Taming the Advice Monster

**Where it appeared**:
- When Alex mentioned team dependency (Sarah wanted to say "Delegate!")
- When Alex identified being a bottleneck (Sarah wanted to give time management tips)
- When discussing solutions (Sarah wanted to suggest RACI matrices)

**How Sarah tamed it**:
- Noticed the urge
- Took a breath
- Asked a question instead

### 2. The Power of "And What Else?"

**Used 7 times in 15 minutes**:
1. After initial problem statement → Engineering/marketing conflict
2. → Team readiness concerns
3. → Being the only one who sees the whole picture
4. (One more check) → "That's it"
5. Applied to the bottleneck issue → Slowing things down
6. → People aren't developing
7. Applied to the pattern → Trust issues

**What it revealed**: The real issue (control and how Alex defines their value) took 7 iterations to reach.

### 3. Staying Curious Longer

**Moments of curiosity**:
- "Say more about that" (open-ended)
- "What pattern do you see?" (exploring)
- "What's hard about that?" (going deeper)
- "What's important about being in control?" (finding core belief)

**Result**: Uncovered insights Alex wouldn't have reached if Sarah had rushed to solutions.

### 4. Making It Personal (The Focus Question)

**The shift**:
- From: "The timeline is tight and teams aren't aligned" (external, can't control)
- To: "I'm the bottleneck" (personal, can control)

**Why it matters**: You can only help with challenges that belong to the person you're coaching.

### 5. Question Follow-ups

Sarah combined questions effectively:
- "And what else do you want?" (AWE + Foundation)
- "And what else are you saying no to?" (AWE + Strategic)
- "Is there anything else I can do for you?" (Critical follow-up to Lazy)

### 6. Silence

**Key moments Sarah stayed silent**:
- After asking questions (giving space to think)
- After Alex identified insights (letting them land)
- After the final learning question (not adding more advice)

**Why it matters**: Silence creates space for insight.

### 7. The Learning Loop

By ending with "What was most useful?":
- Alex explicitly identified the insight (pattern recognition)
- The learning became conscious and memorable
- Sarah learned what actually helped (not what she thought would help)

## Key Takeaways from This Conversation

### 1. The Questions Build on Each Other

The seven questions aren't random - they create a progression:
```
What's on your mind? (Open)
    ↓
And what else? (Deepen)
    ↓
What's the real challenge? (Focus)
    ↓
What do you want? (Clarify)
    ↓
How can I help? (Define)
    ↓
What are you saying no to? (Make conscious)
    ↓
What was most useful? (Extract learning)
```

### 2. "And What Else?" Is the Workhorse

Used 7 times in this conversation, it:
- Prevented premature solutions
- Uncovered deeper issues
- Bought time (Sarah didn't need to have answers)
- Led to the real insight (control = value)

### 3. Coaching Takes Discipline

**Sarah had to**:
- Resist giving advice (multiple times)
- Sit with silence (let Alex think)
- Ask one more question (when she thought she knew the answer)
- Trust the process (even when uncertain)

### 4. The Learning Happens in the Client

**Alex discovered**:
- A pattern, not just a problem
- An underlying belief (control = value)
- Their own solutions (team meeting, email)
- A new definition of leadership

**Sarah didn't tell Alex any of this - the questions led Alex to discover it.**

### 5. Time-Efficient

**15 minutes** produced:
- Deep insight
- Concrete actions
- Identified pattern
- Shifted mindset

Traditional problem-solving meeting might take longer and produce less change.

### 6. The End is Clean

Sarah didn't:
- Add more advice after the learning question
- Suggest additional things to try
- Diminish the insight
- Make it about herself

She ended with "Great. Thanks." - letting the learning be the last word.

---

## Final Note

This conversation demonstrates the **magic of coaching questions**: By staying curious, asking one more question, and resisting the urge to solve, you help people discover insights they'll actually use and remember.

**The paradox**: By doing less (not giving advice), you achieve more (lasting change).

**Your practice**: Find someone today and start with "What's on your mind?" Follow with "And what else?" See where it goes.

**Remember**: You don't need to be perfect. Just ask one more question before giving advice. That's enough to start building the habit.
